# Reflecting: structure and purpose of the work

Reflection is where a finished solution is turned into something the student keeps. The work develops both the solution itself and the student's ability to solve future problems.

## Where to start

From the solution, complete and correct in each detail.

## What the work consists of

Four components. Not all are needed for every problem, but each yields a distinct kind of learning.

1. **Check the result.** Test it against independent expectations: limiting cases, symmetry, dimensional consistency, behavior under input changes, comparison to an analogous simpler problem.
2. **Check the argument.** Revisit the steps the student was least sure of and any assumptions that were convenient to postpone during execution.
3. **Derive the result differently.** A second route, ideally shorter or more direct, builds conviction and often reveals structure the first path hid. Try to see the whole solution at a glance; simplify details and shorten longer parts where possible.
4. **Use the result or method elsewhere.** Ask whether the result applies to a related situation, or whether the method transfers to a different problem. Articulate the general idea behind the approach in problem-independent terms. This is where transfer happens, and transfer is the payoff of the whole process.

## What this phase produces

In the moment: possibly a new and better solution, possibly new facts noticed only on review. Over time, with the habit of surveying solutions this way: knowledge that is well ordered and ready to use, and a stronger ability to solve future problems. Reflection is open-ended; a solution is never fully exhausted.
