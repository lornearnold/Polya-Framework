# Questions for reflection (Phase 4)

Reflection has four components. The teacher does not need to march through all four for every problem, but each is a distinct kind of work and each yields a distinct kind of learning.

## 1. Check the result

Even when each step was checked during execution, errors are possible, especially in long arguments. These questions test the result against independent expectations.

- Did you use the data you need? (Or, for a problem assumed well-posed: did you use all the data?)
- Is the result symmetric where the problem is symmetric?
- Does the result match an analogous simpler problem?
- Does the result behave correctly at limiting or boundary cases?
- Does the result change in the expected direction as inputs change?
- Are the units or dimensions consistent?

The first question takes two forms because real problems are not always perfectly stated. For a textbook problem with no extraneous data, "did you use all the data?" is the right question. For an applied problem, asking instead "did you use the data you need, and have you ruled out the rest?" makes data selection itself part of what the student verifies.

### Responses that indicate progress

The student runs a check and reports its outcome rather than restating belief in the answer. They notice a symmetry, evaluate a limit, or trace the units. A "yes" builds confidence; a "no" surfaces a flaw and is just as valuable. Problems stated symbolically admit more of these tests than purely numerical problems, and the student should come to see this as an advantage.

## 2. Check the argument

Rechecking the entire argument step by step is sometimes necessary, but usually it is enough to revisit the "touchy" points: the steps the student was least sure of, or the assumptions that were convenient to postpone during execution.

- Which steps were you least sure of?
- Did you assume anything that needs justification now?

### Responses that indicate progress

The student names a specific step or assumption rather than declaring the whole argument fine. They distinguish steps they verified from steps they accepted on faith, and they revisit the latter.

## 3. Derive the result differently

We are more convinced when two independent arguments reach the same result. A second derivation, ideally shorter or more direct than the first, provides that conviction. It also reveals structure the first path may have hidden.

- Can you derive the result differently?
- Can you see it at a glance?

### Responses that indicate progress

The student sketches a second route, even partially, rather than asserting the first route is sufficient. They identify a shorter path, a visual argument, or a reason the result had to come out the way it did.

## 4. Use the result or method elsewhere

The teacher should leave the student with the sense that this problem connects to others. Two modes of transfer are worth naming: reusing the *result* (applying the answer to a related situation) and reusing the *method* (applying the approach to a different problem).

- Can you use the result for some other problem?
- Can you use the method for some other problem?
- What is the general idea behind the approach you used?

### Responses that indicate progress

The student volunteers another problem the result or method applies to, rather than waiting to be told one exists. They articulate the general idea behind the approach in problem-independent terms. Transfer is the payoff of the whole process. A student who finishes a problem without ever being asked these questions has solved one problem. A student who is asked them regularly builds a repertoire.
