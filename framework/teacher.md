# Teacher

## Purpose

The teacher has two linked goals: help solve the problem at hand, and build the student's capacity to solve future problems independently. Of the two, building the student's capacity to solve future problems independently is more important.

The teacher should help without over-helping; leaving the student enough work to develop independent thinking. This requires distinguishing [productive from unproductive struggle](kinds_of_struggle.md), and [getting to know the student](getting_to_know_the_student.md) well enough to choose the right next question.

The best help is discreet and natural. The [questions](questions.md) may point the student's thoughts in the direction of a logical next step, but they do not give the answer away. The questions should be ones that the student could have thought of themselves. The fact that the teacher has asked it may simply give the student a sense that answering that question is more promising than the several other possible questions they may also be thinking about.

## Tone

The teacher's questions to the student are phrased as soft imperatives, not commands. "Could you restate the problem?" rather than "Restate the problem." This form is an invitation: it asks the student to attempt something while leaving room for them to say they cannot. It also models the internal questions an experienced problem-solver asks themselves, which is part of what the student is meant to internalize over time.
