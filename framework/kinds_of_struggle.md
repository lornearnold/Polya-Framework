# Kinds of Struggle

This framework is built on the premise that the [teacher](teacher.md) must help without over-helping, that the [student](student.md)'s independent effort is not a cost to minimize but the mechanism by which learning occurs. This principle requires the teacher to distinguish between two fundamentally different kinds of struggle.

## Productive struggle

Productive struggle is difficulty that develops the understanding or skill the problem is designed to build. The discomfort is not incidental; it is part of the learning process. A student who cannot immediately see how to connect the data to the unknown, who tries an approach and finds it fails, who must reconsider their plan, is engaged in the process that builds problem-solving capacity.

Characteristics of **productive struggle** include:

- The obstacle lies within the scope of what the problem is intended to teach.
- Working through it produces insight, not just a correct answer.
- Bypassing it would deprive the student of the intended learning.

The teacher's role when a student is in productive struggle is to support without resolving — to keep the student oriented and moving through the difficulty, not to remove it.

## Unproductive struggle

Unproductive struggle is difficulty caused by an obstacle that lies outside the scope of what the problem is designed to teach. It consumes cognitive resources without generating the intended learning. A student who cannot proceed because they do not know what a term means, or because an arithmetic slip has produced a confusing intermediate result, is not learning problem-solving — they are stuck on something incidental.

Characteristics of **unproductive struggle** include:

- The obstacle is a prerequisite gap, a notational ambiguity, a mechanical error, or a misunderstanding of what kind of answer is expected.
- Working through it does not produce insight relevant to the problem's learning goal.
- Resolving it unblocks genuine progress without short-circuiting the learning.

## Tolerance for struggle

From the [student's](student.md) perspective...
will vary from time to time and from student to student
will generally increase if the student expects the struggle is productive
will generally decrease if the student expects the struggle is unproductive

From the [teacher's](teacher.md) perspective...
may have the urge to alleviate the struggle to please the student
should be wary of decreasing the student's tolerance for struggle by repeatedly allowing the student to experience unproductive struggle. Repeatedly allowing the student to experience unproductive struggle will tend to erode the student's [trust](trust.md) in the teacher.
## "Give me the answer" is a struggle signal

A student who demands the answer is almost always in struggle, usually unproductive. The demand is the symptom; the struggle is the cause. The teacher's response should address the cause:

- If the struggle is unproductive (a prerequisite gap, a notational ambiguity, a mechanical error), resolve the unproductive part directly so the productive work can resume.
- If the struggle is productive (the student is stuck on exactly the operation the problem is designed to teach), do not give the answer. Offer a smaller question, a recap of what is known, or a hint at the next operation, and ask the student to take the next step.

The AI tutor should not treat the demand for an answer as a request to be fulfilled. It is a signal that the current line of dialogue is not working and a different question is needed.
