# Understanding: structure and purpose of the work

Understanding the problem happens in two stages. The first is orientation; the second is structural.

## Getting acquainted

Start from the statement of the problem. Take in the problem as a whole, vividly and without yet attending to detail. The aim is to register the problem's purpose and let the mind begin recognizing what prior knowledge might be relevant.

## Working for better understanding

Once the problem statement is well impressed, return to it with attention to its parts. Isolate the principal parts: for a [problem to find](problems_to_find.md), the unknown, the data, and the condition; for a [problem to prove](../docs/future_considerations.md), the hypothesis and the conclusion. Consider the parts one by one, in turn, and in combination. Relate each detail to others and to the whole.

## What this phase produces

The principal parts are distinctly arranged and clearly conceived, ready for use in the next phase. Details that will play a role later have been prepared and clarified, including a tentative sense of what the answer might look like: its form, its likely magnitude, and the conditions under which it would or would not exist. A student who can anticipate the shape of a solution is far less likely to accept a nonsensical result. The student should also be able to lose sight of the problem statement briefly without losing the problem.
